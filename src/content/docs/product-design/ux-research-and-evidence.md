# UX research and evidence

UX research reduces uncertainty about users, their context, their goals, and how a design performs in use. The research question should determine the method.

## Match evidence to the question

Qualitative evidence helps explain behavior, language, expectations, and failure patterns. Quantitative evidence helps estimate frequency, distribution, magnitude, and change when the measurement is valid.

Neither type is inherently stronger. A large metric can show that abandonment increased without explaining why. A small set of interviews can reveal plausible causes without establishing how common they are.

Useful evidence can include observation, interviews, surveys, usability sessions, support records, operational data, search logs, task metrics, and experiments.

## Interviews and surveys

Interviews are useful for understanding experiences, motivations, vocabulary, constraints, and remembered events. They are weaker evidence for exact future behavior or precise frequency.

Surveys can collect structured responses from more people, but question wording, sampling, non-response, and self-report bias can distort results. A percentage is not representative unless the sample and measurement support that inference.

## Separate observation from interpretation

Record what was observed before turning it into a design conclusion. Distinguish a participant statement, an observed behavior, a measured event, and the team's explanation of that evidence.

Look for contradictory evidence rather than only examples that support the current design.

## Research ethically

Collect only data needed for the research purpose. Protect personal or sensitive information, make participation informed, and avoid exposing participants to unnecessary risk.

## Limits

Research does not eliminate judgment. Samples are incomplete, behavior changes with context, and some questions cannot be answered directly by users. Use several forms of evidence when the decision cost justifies it.
