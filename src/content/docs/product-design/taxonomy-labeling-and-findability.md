# Taxonomy, labeling, and findability

A taxonomy defines categories and relationships. Labels expose those concepts to users. Findability is the ability to locate an item or answer through the available paths.

## Design categories around distinctions users can apply

Categories should be mutually understandable even when they cannot be mathematically exclusive. If users repeatedly cannot predict where an item belongs, the taxonomy or labels need review.

Avoid categories such as "Other" as a permanent home for unrelated high-value content. They hide the limits of the classification system.

## Use labels that predict content

A label should help users anticipate what they will find after selecting it. Prefer domain terms users understand over internal team names or clever wording.

Keep one term for one concept. Add aliases or redirects when important alternate terminology exists instead of renaming the concept in different parts of the product.

## Evaluate findability through tasks

Findability can be tested with navigation tasks, tree testing, search behavior, support evidence, and observation. A category structure that looks logical to its authors can still fail users who approach from a different model.

## Support more than one path when needed

Navigation, search, contextual links, and indexes can complement each other. Multiple paths are useful when they converge on one canonical item rather than creating duplicated copies that drift.

## Limits

Taxonomies evolve as domains and user language change. Stability has value because links and learned paths accumulate, so rename or restructure only when the expected findability gain justifies migration cost.
