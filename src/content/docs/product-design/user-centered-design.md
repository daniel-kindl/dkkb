# User-centered design

User-centered design treats an explicit understanding of users, tasks, and context as an input to design rather than assuming that the development team already knows what people need.

The process is iterative: understand the context, identify user needs, produce design solutions, and evaluate those solutions against the needs and constraints that motivated them.

## Why it matters

A system can be technically correct and still be difficult to understand, inefficient to use, or poorly matched to the work people are trying to complete.

User-centered design reduces that risk by making design decisions answerable to evidence about real use rather than only internal preference.

## Practical implications

Useful practices include:

- identifying the people who will use or be affected by the system;
- understanding their goals, tasks, environment, and constraints;
- involving representative users where practical;
- evaluating designs before implementation cost becomes difficult to reverse;
- revisiting assumptions when evidence contradicts the current design.

## Limits

User-centered design does not mean implementing every user request. Users can disagree, describe solutions instead of needs, or optimize for their current workflow.

Design still requires judgment across user needs, accessibility, technical constraints, business constraints, safety, and long-term product coherence.
